# Playbook: the psychology of clicks and retention

The why behind the storyboard skill. Each principle ends with **Use it**, which says how it appears
in the story. When you explain a choice to the user, name the principle in a few words.

---

## Part 1: Why people click

### Curiosity gap (information-gap theory, Loewenstein)
Curiosity is the discomfort of noticing a specific gap between what we know and what we want to know.
It needs **some** knowledge (a scenario we can picture) plus a **specific** unknown. A vague topic
("zombie survival") opens no gap; a precise situation with a missing piece does ("what's on the
other side of that door?").
- **Use it:**
  - The thumbnail shows the situation; the missing piece is the outcome or the threat's identity.
  - The title adds context, never the answer.

### Stakes and loss aversion
Losses weigh more than equal gains. A dependent, a countdown or a dwindling resource makes the cost of
failure concrete.
- **Use it:** a premise names what can be lost: a child, the air supply, the last 40 gallons.

### High-concept premise
The premise is understood in one line and implies conflict by itself: **familiar scenario + sharp
constraint + vulnerable dependent or cost + a number**.
- *Surviving a zombie outbreak* → *Surviving 365 Days Underground With My Toddler*
- *Lost at sea* → *I Drifted 76 Days on a Life Raft With 3 Days of Water*
- *Haunted house* → *We Had 30 Days to Sell a House That Wouldn't Let Us Leave*

Constraints that raise drama: a time limit, a confined space, a dependent, a body problem (injury,
illness, pregnancy), a rule you can't break (no noise, no light), a scarce resource, isolation.

### Premise scorecard (1–5 each; recommend premises at 28+ out of 35)
| Criterion | 5 means |
|---|---|
| **Curiosity gap** | One specific, burning question the viewer can't answer without watching |
| **Stakes** | A vulnerable dependent or an irreversible loss, felt at once |
| **Specificity** | Numbers, a time span, a place: "365 days", "500 gallons", "Day 214" |
| **Identification** | The viewer can imagine being there ("what would I do?"), with an ordinary protagonist |
| **Novelty** | A fresh twist on a known genre: familiar enough to understand, new enough to want |
| **Escalation fuel** | At least 5 distinct problems of different kinds, enough for the length |
| **Visual clarity for AI** | Few recurring locations and characters, strong single images, no text needed in pictures |

### Packaging rules (title + thumbnail)
1. **One idea.** Thumbnail and title tell one story together. The thumbnail poses the question, and
   the title gives the frame (who, how long, what constraint).
2. **They complement each other; they never repeat.** If the thumbnail shows a bunker door, the title
   doesn't say "bunker door". It says *Surviving 365 Days Underground*.
3. **Thumbnail:**
   - **One focal subject** at least a third of the frame, **at most 3 elements**, strong contrast
     (light against dark, warm against cold), and one accent colour (a red alert light) on the subject.
   - A visible question: a threat half-seen, a door, a gauge in the red, a face reacting to something
     out of frame.
   - Must read on a phone at about 160 px wide: no small details, and no text in the generated image.
4. **Thumbnail text** (optional, added in the edit): ≤ 3 words, a number or an ominous fragment ("DAY
   214", "DON'T OPEN IT"), never the title.
5. **Title:** ≤ 55 characters, so it isn't cut off. It leads with the premise and a number; no
   ALL CAPS and no emoji clutter. The first person ("I", "We") increases identification in story
   niches.
6. **The promise test:** write one line saying what a viewer expects after clicking. If the video
   doesn't show it within 5 seconds and pay it off by the end, change the packaging or the story.

## Part 2: Why people keep watching

### The first 30 seconds
The opening decides whether YouTube keeps showing the video: viewers who leave early tell it the
packaging over-promised.
- **0–5 s, confirm the promise.** The viewer sees what they clicked for: the bunker, the raft, the
  door.
- **By 15 s, the stakes.** Who can be lost, and what's running out.
- **By 30 s, plant the central open loop.** A mystery answered only at the end ("Nobody knew we were
  down here. So who was knocking?").
- **No friction.** No greeting, no channel intro, no "in this video", no sponsor, no slow establishing
  montage.
- **Two openings that work:**
  - *In medias res:* the worst moment, then "Three months earlier…".
  - *Warmth before the break:* an ordinary ritual (Tuesday pancake day) that the emergency destroys.
    The warmth makes the loss land.

### Open loops (Zeigarnik effect)
People remember and are bothered by unfinished things. An unanswered question keeps attention until
it's closed.
- **Use it:**
  - Always have at least one loop open.
  - Nest them: 1–2 big ones across the video, plus small ones closed within a minute or two.
  - Close one only as another opens: answer the question and reveal a worse one in the same beat.
  - A loop never paid off feels like a cheat. Close every loop, or make the open ending deliberate.

### "But / therefore", not "and then"
Scenes linked by causality pull the viewer forward; lists of events don't. Each beat follows from
the last ("therefore") or reverses it ("but").
- **Use it:** check each beat's link to the next. "And then" means a beat to cut or connect.

### Escalation: problem → escalation → tactical solution → cost
Every episode raises the stakes in a **new dimension**: external threat, inside the shelter, the
body (illness, injury), resources, the group (conflict, betrayal), the mind (paranoia,
hallucination), and moral choices (let someone in or not).
- **Use it:**
  - Each solution costs something (fuel, trust, a risky trip outside), and that cost feeds the next
    problem.
  - Never repeat a kind of problem at the same level.

### Specificity and concreteness
Concrete details are easier to picture, remember and believe (processing fluency). "500 gallons in
food-grade tanks on the concrete slab, about 5.5 months" feels real; "lots of water" feels made up.
Numbers also give the viewer **progress meters** to follow (gallons left, CO₂ ppm, days), and people
push harder as a goal gets closer (goal-gradient effect), so those meters pull attention through the
middle.
- **Use it:**
  - Each episode has at least one number and one procedure ("we taped the vents, moved the generator
    to the far wall, and timed the fan: 4 minutes an hour").
  - Show the numbers as Title cards ("CO₂: 1,800 PPM").

### Identification and anchor objects
We care about specific, vulnerable people with small, real habits. Objects make feelings visible: the
stuffed rabbit, the medicine labelled in Mom's handwriting, the calendar with crossed-off days.
- **Use it:**
  - Introduce the dependent early, in a warm moment.
  - Each anchor object comes back in danger (the rabbit left upstairs) and in the resolution.

### Dread beats surprise (Hitchcock's bomb under the table)
Showing the audience a danger the characters can't fully escape creates minutes of suspense; a
sudden shock gives one second.
- **Use it:**
  - Let the viewer know about the threat before it strikes: footsteps above while the toddler is
    about to cry.
  - Stretch the moment with silence and a slow push in. Use the jump scare rarely.

### Pattern interrupts (orienting response)
Attention drops when stimulation is uniform; a change in sound or picture briefly re-captures it.
- **Use it:**
  - A change every 45–90 s, and vary the kind:
    - a time-jump Title card;
    - a hard cut to silence, or radio static cutting out;
    - a whisper;
    - a sudden sound event;
    - a wide shot switching to a tight close-up of a gauge or an object;
    - a light camera Shake;
    - a short video clip.
  - The picture changes every 3–6 s in tense stretches (scenes and shots).

### Emotional rhythm (tension and release)
Constant maximum tension numbs the viewer. Short relief makes the next drop feel bigger.
- **Use it:** alternate: a small win, or a warm moment with the dependent, comes right before the
  next escalation.

### Peak-end rule
People judge an experience mostly by its most intense moment and its end. The ending decides the
like, comment and next click.
- **Use it:**
  - The climax is the peak.
  - The final twist recontextualises an early setup ("the voice knew Theo's name").
  - The last line lingers or points to the next video.
  - End fast after the payoff: no long outro.

## Part 3: Structure by length

| Length | Hook | Episodes | Turning points |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shorts (30–60 s)** | 0–2 s: the image *is* the hook; first line poses the question | 1 problem, 1 escalation | Twist in the last 5 s, ideally looping back to the first line |
| **6–10 min** | 0–30 s, as above | 4–5 episodes of 60–120 s | Midpoint reversal (~50%), lowest point (~75%), climax, twist |
| **12–20 min** | 0–30 s, as above, plus a 30–60 s "stakes setup" (resource math, the rules) | 6–8 episodes of 90–150 s | Same, plus a second, smaller reversal at ~30% |

Episodes in day-count stories jump in time (Day 1 → 12 → 88 → 214 → 365). Each jump is a free
pattern interrupt and promises the viewer progress.

## Part 4: Retention audit checklist

Check the finished storyboard against each of these:
1. **Promise:** the first 5 s show what the title and thumbnail promised.
2. **Hook:** stakes by 15 s; the central loop planted by 30 s; nothing in the first 30 s that could be
   cut.
3. **Loop ledger:** at least one loop is always open; every loop closes (or is a deliberate cliffhanger);
   the central loop closes in the last 15%.
4. **Causality:** every beat is a "but" or a "therefore" to the one before.
5. **Escalation:** each episode is worse in a new dimension; each solution has a cost.
6. **Specificity:** at least one number and one procedure per episode; anchor objects set up and paid off.
7. **Interrupts:** none of the gaps between pattern interrupts is over 90 s, and they vary in kind.
8. **Pictures:** a new picture every ≤ 6 s in tense stretches and ≤ 8 s elsewhere; shot sizes vary.
9. **Voice:** no dead air over ~8 s without a planned reason; no line that only repeats what the
   picture shows.
10. **Rhythm:** tension and relief alternate; there's no stretch of flat maximum tension longer than ~2 min.
11. **Ending:** the twist pays off an earlier setup; the video ends within ~20 s of it.
12. **Honesty:** the packaging is paid off, and fiction or dramatisation is clear.
